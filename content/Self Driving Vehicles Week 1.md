As with the other presentation of the first week, I was not able to attend. However, I imagine the topic itself to be quite interesting.  
I struggle a bit to see the direct relevance to IT Strategy, but I can understand how issue that arise within the topic of self-driving cars can be applied to other organizations and use cases.  
Self driving cars are interesting, because they exemplify ethical challenges of AI usage. Everybody has heard of the dilemma of AI engineers trying to program a car. In a crisis situation, should it protect the passenger, or minimize the damage?  
I am not entirely sure how the issue is currently handled, but I could imagine that the priority is to protect the passenger.  
In any case this applies to a number of different AI scenarios - such as medical AI systems, therapy chatbots, AI Companions. The underlying challenge is one of accountability - who is accountable when things go wrong? And how can organizations reduce their exposure to possible risks?  
These were my thought before seeing the slides. I did not prepare more, since I was not sure how the topic would be approached (technical, IT strategy focus, or ethical?).

### What did I learn?

I did not know that there are real autonomy levels, but it makes sense, seeing as there is a spectrum to the autonomy features in cars.  
Also, I thought that maps would play a larger role in self-driving cars. However I can see how real time information is much more valuable. Maybe this could even link back to the issue of [[Digital Provenance Week 1|Digital Provenance]] - since real time information is usually most authentic and correct. With maps and updates you run the risks of missing information or running on outdated, potentially false information.  
Next to technical part, the presentation also includes insights on the current legal framework and the accompanying challenges that I already talked about in the first part of this learning diary.  
I am quite happy to learn that Germany is cautious regarding the usage of self driving systems.  
Considering the ethical dilemma, I think it is quite difficult to get to any bottom here - I would have wished to see some transformative move to other contexts of the same liability issue and how companies currently handle the issue. I can understand that a dilemma is not solvable but at the same time companies need to have some kind of process to handle the dilemma. As such it remains descriptive - "The dilemma is there, and it exists also in other contexts".
